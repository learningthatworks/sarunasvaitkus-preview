Isometric
3D

Tiny 3D worlds with no vanishing point. Every edge runs at 30 degrees, so objects keep their size wherever they sit, and a whole city fits neatly on one screen.

Open the kit Copy the prompt

What defines it

Isometric design shows three sides of an object at once without perspective. It feels like a tidy model on a table, which makes complex systems easy to explain.

Fixed 30 degree angles

Horizontal edges run at 30 degrees up to the left and right. Verticals stay vertical.

No vanishing point

Objects far away are drawn the same size as objects nearby, so the scene reads like a diagram.

Three shades per object

One light top face, one mid face and one dark face. That trio creates depth with flat colour.

Stacked extrusion

Buttons and cards get a stepped shadow at 45 degrees, as if they were thick blocks.

Where it came from

Isometric drawing began as an engineering tool and became a favourite of games and tech illustration.

  1. 1822

    A drawing method

    Cambridge professor William Farish describes isometric projection as a way to draw machines accurately.

  2. 1982

    Arcade depth

    Sega's Zaxxon is one of the first arcade games to use an isometric view.

  3. 1990s

    City builders

    Strategy and simulation games use isometric maps to show whole cities and bases at once.

  4. 2014

    Impossible geometry

    The mobile game Monument Valley turns isometric illusions into art and wins design awards.

  5. 2010s+

    Tech illustration

    Product pages and explainers use isometric scenes to show servers, workflows and data flows.

Motion study

Isometric motion stacks and assembles. Blocks drop into place along the vertical axis and the angles never change.

Each block drops straight down with a small bounce. Keep all movement on the three isometric axes.

Prompt it

A ready prompt for AI design and coding tools. It names exact tokens and bans the usual defaults, so the output keeps this style's point of view.

Design a responsive landing page for a cloud hosting service for small teams in an authentic Isometric 3D style.

Palette: Paper #F1F4FA, Top face #FFFFFF, Mid face #C9D6F2, Shade face #93A9E0, Coral #FF6B4A, Teal #2BB3A3, Ink #1C2440.
Typography: Chivo 800 for headings, Chivo 400 for body, Chivo Mono for labels.
Layout: Two-column hero: headline left, an isometric city of stacked blocks on the right drawn in SVG with true 30 degree projection. Light isometric line pattern in the page background.
Signature details: Every object uses three shades (top light, left mid, right dark), consistent light direction, stepped 45 degree block shadows on cards and buttons, 2px ink borders.
Components: Chunky buttons that lift on hover and press flat on click, cards with 8px stepped shadows, mono badges, tables with block shadows.
Motion: Blocks drop along the vertical axis with a slight bounce, staggered.
Avoid: perspective or vanishing points, mixed projections, blurred shadows, tilted body text, purple gradients.
Do not fall back on generic AI defaults: no centered hero with a gradient blob, no identical rounded cards with soft grey shadows, no purple-to-blue gradients, no emoji icons, unless this style explicitly calls for them.
Accessibility: body text contrast of at least 4.5:1, visible keyboard focus on every control, labels on all form fields, and respect prefers-reduced-motion.
Deliver one self-contained HTML file with CSS variables for every color, font, radius and shadow token.

Common mistakes

Where this style usually goes wrong, and how to keep it honest.

Wrong angles

Mixing 30 degree and 45 degree views in one scene looks broken. Pick one projection.

Shading chaos

If the light face changes side between objects, the world stops making sense.

Tiny details

Isometric scenes shrink fast on phones. Keep objects chunky and labels outside the drawing.

Everything tilted

Tilted text is hard to read. Keep headings and body text flat.

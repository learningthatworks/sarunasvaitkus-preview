A print look made of a few bright spot inks. Colours overlap into new ones, grain shows through, and the layers never line up perfectly, which is exactly the charm.

Open the kit Copy prompt

What defines it

Risograph printing uses one drum per ink colour. Designers love its fluorescent inks, visible grain and slightly shifted layers, and the web version copies them with blend modes.

Spot colour inks

Two to four bold inks, often fluorescent pink, blue and yellow, instead of full colour.

Overprinting

Where inks overlap they mix into new colours. On screen, mix-blend-mode: multiply does the same.

Misregistration

Each colour layer lands slightly off. Offsets of a few pixels make the print feel real.

Grain and texture

Paper texture and uneven ink coverage add warmth. A subtle noise layer recreates it.

Where it came from

The Risograph was built as an office duplicator. Artists and zine makers turned it into a design style.

  1. 1946

    A printing company

    Noboru Hayama founds the company in Tokyo that will later make Risograph machines.

  2. 1980

    Print Gocco

    The company releases a small home screen printer that becomes a craft favourite in Japan.

  3. 1986

    The Risograph arrives

    The first Risograph duplicators are sold to schools, churches and offices for cheap bulk printing.

  4. 2000s

    Art school rediscovery

    Designers and illustrators start using old machines for zines, posters and art books.

  5. 2010s+

    Riso culture

    Independent riso studios and zine fairs spread, and the look moves onto websites through CSS blend modes.

Motion study

Riso motion plays with registration. Ink layers slide apart and back together, mixing new colours where they overlap.

The purple, green and orange you see are never drawn. They appear where two inks multiply.

Prompt it

A ready prompt for AI design and coding tools. It names exact tokens and bans the usual defaults, so the output keeps this style's point of view.

Design a responsive landing page for an independent print studio and zine shop in an authentic Risograph style.

Palette: Paper #F4EFE4, Fluorescent pink #FF48B0, Riso blue #0078BF, Yellow #FFE800, Teal #00838A, Ink navy #1F2A44.
Typography: Anton uppercase for headlines, Instrument Sans for body.
Layout: Two-column hero: a huge two-layer headline (blue layer plus pink layer offset a few pixels with multiply blending), and overlapping pink, blue and yellow shapes on the right with a halftone dot layer.
Signature details: Only three spot inks (#FF48B0, #0078BF, #FFE800) on warm paper, mix-blend-mode: multiply for overprinting, slight misregistration, subtle SVG grain texture.
Components: Buttons with an offset ink layer behind them, cards with a multiplied pink or blue ink shadow slightly rotated, yellow highlighter badges, striped two-ink progress bars.
Motion: Ink layers sliding in and out of registration, colours mixing where they overlap.
Avoid: full-colour photos, gradients, pink or yellow text, heavy noise, offsets on small text.

Common mistakes

Where this style usually goes wrong, and how to keep it honest.

Muddy overlaps

Too many overlapping inks turn brown. Stick to two or three.

Offset text

Misregistration on small text makes it unreadable. Only offset big headlines.

Pink text

Fluorescent pink and yellow fail contrast as text colours. Use them for shapes.

Fake grain overload

Heavy noise textures look dirty. Keep grain subtle.

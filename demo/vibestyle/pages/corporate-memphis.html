Corporate Memphis

Flat, faceless people with tiny heads, long bendy limbs and purple skin, waving from every tech homepage around 2020. It looked friendly, then it looked like everyone.

Open the kit Copy prompt
A cautionary page: learn it to know why it stopped working.

What defines it

Corporate Memphis is a flat illustration style that big tech companies adopted almost at once. It teaches a useful lesson: a style can become a template, and templates make brands disappear.

Faceless characters

People have simple heads, few or no facial features and exaggerated long limbs.

Non-realistic skin

Purple, blue or orange skin avoids showing any specific identity.

Blobs and plants

Soft blob shapes and potted plants fill the background.

Friendly geometric type

Round geometric sans-serifs and pastel palettes complete the look.

Where it came from

The style spread so fast that it became shorthand for tech marketing within a few years.

  1. 2017

    A design system for people

    Facebook launches an illustration system called Alegria, created with the studio Buck, with flat bendy characters.

  2. 2018

    Everyone adopts it

    Tech companies, banks and startups use similar characters on homepages, onboarding screens and ads.

  3. 2020

    A nickname appears

    Critics start calling it Corporate Memphis, linking it loosely to the colourful Memphis Design of the 1980s.

  4. 2020s

    Backlash

    Designers mock the sameness, and brands shift toward 3D, photography and hand-made illustration.

Motion study

Corporate Memphis motion is polite. A character waves while a background blob slowly morphs.

Gentle, looping and inoffensive, which is exactly its strength and its weakness.

Prompt it

A ready prompt for AI design and coding tools. It names exact tokens and bans the usual defaults, so the output keeps this style's point of view.

Design a responsive landing page for a team collaboration tool, redesigned to escape the Corporate Memphis cliché in an authentic Corporate Memphis style.

Palette: Warm white #FFF8F2, Violet #5B4BDB, Peach #FFB199, Mint #7ED6B6, Sunny #FFD166, Deep indigo #2B2350.
Typography: Poppins 700 for headings and 400 for body.
Layout: Two-column hero: headline and text left, a flat illustrated figure with long limbs, a tiny head, violet clothing and a mint blob behind it on the right.
Signature details: Flat shapes, no outlines, pastel palette with one violet, large radius, soft shadows, potted plant or blob accents.
Components: Pill buttons, soft cards, mint badges, pill tabs.
Motion: A slow waving arm and a morphing blob.
Avoid: copying known brand illustrations, using the style unedited as a default, faceless crowds as the only human element.
Do not fall back on generic AI defaults: no centered hero with a gradient blob, no identical rounded cards with soft grey shadows, no purple-to-blue gradients, no emoji icons, unless this style explicitly calls for them.
Accessibility: body text contrast of at least 4.5:1, visible keyboard focus on every control, labels on all form fields, and respect prefers-reduced-motion.
Deliver one self-contained HTML file with CSS variables for every color, font, radius and shadow token.

Common mistakes

Where this style usually goes wrong, and how to keep it honest.

Instant sameness

Your brand looks like hundreds of others. If you use it, twist it hard.

Hiding real people

Faceless figures can feel empty. Real photos or specific characters build more trust.

The AI default

AI tools love this look. Using it unedited signals no design decision was made.

Copying other brands

Many companies built their own versions. Borrowing theirs is still copying.

LuxuryType

Luxury brands speak through typography. Hairline serifs, wide-tracked capitals and a great deal of silence suggest craft, heritage and price before a single product appears.

Discover the kit Copy the prompt

What defines it

Luxury typography uses contrast and restraint as signals of value. Few words, big type and generous space say that nothing here needs to shout.

High-contrast serifs

Didone faces with hairline thins and heavy thicks, designed to be seen large.

Tracked capitals

Small uppercase labels with wide letter spacing, used for navigation and details.

Generous silence

Huge margins and very little copy. Space implies that attention is precious.

Muted precious palette

Black, ivory and one metallic accent such as champagne or gold.

Where it came from

The look of luxury comes from late 18th-century type and 20th-century fashion magazines.

  1. 1780s

    Didot

    The Didot family in Paris cuts typefaces with extreme contrast between thick and thin strokes.

  2. 1790s

    Bodoni

    Giambattista Bodoni in Parma refines the same idea. Didone faces become symbols of elegance.

  3. 1930s+

    Fashion mastheads

    Fashion magazines set their names in Didone capitals, linking the style to couture for decades.

  4. 2010s

    The sans wave

    Several fashion houses swap their serif logos for plain sans-serif wordmarks, a trend critics nickname blanding.

  5. 2020s

    Serifs return

    Brands and designers bring back expressive high-contrast serifs to regain character.

Motion study

Luxury motion is slow and deliberate. Letters open up, lines of light pass through, and nothing hurries.

Atelier

Letter spacing is the animation. Seven seconds per cycle keeps it calm.

Common mistakes

Where this style usually goes wrong, and how to keep it honest.

Hairlines at small sizes

Didone thins disappear under 24 pixels. Use them only for display text.

Tracked body text

Wide letter spacing works for labels, not for paragraphs.

Gold everywhere

Metallic accents lose value when overused. One gold element per view.

Luxury as cliché

Black and gold alone looks cheap. The quality comes from spacing, scale and restraint.
